## Cảnh 04 · You are water
Hình: BẢNG (lop-hoc-bang.png): hình người đổ đầy nước tới 60%; trẻ sơ sinh 75%, người cao tuổi 55%; não, tim 73% · phổi 83% · xương 31%; tế bào 2/3 — ngoài 1/3; vòng: chảy, bốc hơi, lọc, thay mới.
> If you poured your body into a jar, most of what filled it would be water.
> In an adult, water makes up about sixty percent of body weight.
> Newborn babies have even more, about seventy-five percent.
> Older adults have less, around fifty-five percent.
> And every organ holds a different amount.
> According to a classic analysis from 1945, the brain and heart are about seventy-three percent water.
> The lungs are about eighty-three percent. Bones, only about thirty-one percent.
> About two-thirds of all that water sits inside your cells.
> The rest is outside: in your blood, and in the spaces between cells.
> And this water never sits still.
> It flows, it evaporates, it gets filtered, and it is replaced every single day.
> To see why, let's follow a drop of water through its three main jobs.

## Cảnh 05 · The delivery river
Hình: BẢNG: mạch máu như con sông, kiện hàng dinh dưỡng và oxy trôi tới tế bào, chiều về chở chất thải; tim đập nhanh dần khi mực nước hạ.
> Job number one: water is your delivery system.
> The liquid part of your blood, called plasma, is mostly water.
> That stream carries nutrients and oxygen to every cell.
> On the way back, it picks up the waste that needs to go.
> In your gut, your body releases around eight liters of digestive fluids every day to process food.
> Then almost all of that water is absorbed back again.
> When you run low on water, your blood volume drops a little.
> And your heart has to work harder to keep the flow from slowing down.

## Cảnh 06 · The air conditioner
Hình: BẢNG: da, giọt mồ hôi bay hơi mang nhiệt; nhiệt kế hạ; mặt trời, người chạy: "2 LITERS / HOUR".
> Job number two: water keeps you from overheating.
> When your body temperature rises, your skin releases sweat.
> As the sweat evaporates, it carries heat away from your body.
> It's like standing in front of a fan with a wet towel on your face.
> The hotter it gets, and the more you move, the more you sweat.
> During hard exercise in the sun, you can lose around two liters of sweat an hour.
> And every drop of that has to be replaced.

## Cảnh 07 · The filter plant
Hình: BẢNG: hai quả thận như nhà máy; "180 L" (bồn tắm), ">99% BACK", "≈1.5 L" nước tiểu; đồng hồ tốc độ có vạch đỏ.
> Job number three: water helps your kidneys clean your blood.
> Every day, your two kidneys filter about one hundred and eighty liters of fluid from your blood.
> That's almost a full bathtub.
> But more than ninety-nine percent of it is absorbed back.
> Only about one and a half liters becomes urine, carrying waste out of your body.
> Your kidneys can also adjust: hold on to water when you're short, let more go when you have extra.
> But this filter has a speed limit.
> Remember that detail. It will matter later.

## Cảnh 08 · The water scale
Hình: BẢNG: cân đĩa. Trái "OUT": nước tiểu, mồ hôi, hơi thở. Phải "IN": đồ uống, thức ăn. "≈ 2–3 L / DAY".
> Now, let's put everything on a scale.
> On the left is the water you lose: through urine, through sweat, and with every breath.
> Even sitting still in an air-conditioned room, you're still losing water.
> On the right is the water you take in: from drinks, and from food too.
> Your body even makes a little water when it burns food, but that part is very small.
> For adults, the daily need is around two to three liters, food included.
> As soon as the two sides fall out of balance, your body reacts.

## Cảnh 09 · The sensor
Hình: BẢNG: não với cảm biến kim đo DILUTE ↔ CONCENTRATED; "+1–2%" → đèn vàng: ADH xuống thận; đèn đỏ: "THIRSTY!"; ly nước, kim về giữa; cụ già, đèn mờ.
> Because deep inside your brain, there's a sensor.
> It constantly checks whether your blood is diluted or concentrated.
> It works like the thermostat on an air conditioner, except it measures water instead of temperature.
> When you're short on water, your blood gets more concentrated.
> Just one to two percent more, and the sensor switches on a warning light.
> First, your brain releases a hormone called ADH.
> ADH tells your kidneys to hold on to water. You pee less, and it gets darker.
> If that's not enough, your brain sends a second signal: thirst.
> You drink, your blood is diluted again, and the warning light goes off.
> In healthy people, this system runs quietly, day and night.
> But in older adults, thirst often arrives later, and weaker.

## Cảnh 10 · Running low
Hình: BẢNG: nhóm nữ "−1.4%": đau đầu, mệt, khó tập trung; nhóm nam: tỉnh táo và trí nhớ giảm; "RESULTS STILL MIXED"; người chạy "−2% → LESS ENDURANCE"; cuối: cậu bé (dau-dau) lúc 3 giờ chiều.
> So what happens when you ignore these signals?
> In one experiment, healthy young women were made mildly dehydrated.
> They lost about one point four percent of their body weight.
> They got headaches, felt tired, found it harder to focus, and everything felt like more effort.
> Another experiment, in men, found they were less alert.
> Their working memory dipped as well.
> The results across studies don't fully agree yet.
> But for exercise, the picture is clearer.
> Losing more than two percent of your body weight to dehydration can reduce your endurance.
> Especially in the heat.
> Severe dehydration, like from long bouts of diarrhea or vomiting, can be dangerous.
> Then you need to replace both water and salts, with an oral rehydration solution, and see a doctor right away.
> Headache. Sluggish. Hard to focus.
> Sound familiar? That's you, at three in the afternoon.

## Cảnh 13 · The key
Hình: Bong bóng suy nghĩ: cân thăng bằng; "A FIXED NUMBER" bị gạch, "BALANCE" in đè.
> The key is the scale.
> Your body doesn't need a fixed number. It needs balance: water in equals water out.
> And it already has a built-in sensor to keep that balance.
> So where did the eight-glasses number come from?

## Cảnh 14 · The eight-glasses myth
Hình: BẢNG: báo cáo cũ "1945" — "≈ 2.5 L / DAY" sáng lên, dòng "MOSTLY ALREADY IN FOOD" bị gấp góc; kính lúp "2002 · VALTIN"; hai ly "WOMEN 2.0 L · MEN 2.5 L — FOOD INCLUDED" (EFSA); "US: 2.7 L · 3.7 L".
> Eight glasses, at about two hundred and fifty milliliters each, comes to about two liters.
> In 1945, a nutrition board in the United States made a recommendation.
> Each person needs about two and a half liters of water a day.
> But in the very next sentence, they wrote that most of this amount is already in your food.
> In 2002, the physiologist Heinz Valtin traced the origin of the eight-glasses advice.
> He suggested that the second sentence had simply been forgotten.
> And he found no scientific evidence that everyone must drink exactly eight glasses.
> Today, the European Food Safety Authority gives a reference value.
> About two liters a day for women.
> For men, about two and a half liters.
> That's total water, including the water in food, under normal conditions.
> In the United States, the reference values are higher.
> About two point seven liters for women, and three point seven liters for men.
> The numbers differ because each is based on different data.
> They reflect how much water healthy people there typically take in each day.

## Cảnh 15 · Where water comes from
Hình: BẢNG: đĩa thức ăn (dưa hấu, bát canh, rau, cơm) "≈ 1/5 – 1/3"; ly cà phê ✓ "IN MODERATION"; thang màu nước tiểu 5 ô: vàng rơm nhạt ✓ … vàng sẫm "DRINK MORE".
> So where does your water come from?
> About a fifth to a third comes from food: vegetables, fruit, soup, rice.
> Watermelon, for example, is more than ninety percent water.
> Tea and coffee count too.
> A study in 2014 followed men who were regular coffee drinkers.
> At four cups a day, coffee hydrated them about as well as plain water.
> Soft drinks and juice contain water too, but they come with a lot of sugar and calories.
> So plain water is still the best choice for everyday thirst.
> And to check whether you're drinking enough, there's a simple trick: look at the color of your pee.
> Pale straw yellow usually means you're well hydrated.
> Dark yellow, like strong tea, is a sign you should drink more.
> Some medicines and vitamins can change the color too, so treat this as a hint, not a test.

## Cảnh 16 · When to drink more
Hình: BẢNG: biểu tượng lần lượt — mặt trời, giày chạy, nhiệt kế sốt, bà bầu, cụ già; đồng hồ tốc độ vượt vạch đỏ; "LOW SODIUM"; huy chương "BOSTON 2002 · 13%"; "HEART OR KIDNEY DISEASE: FOLLOW YOUR DOCTOR".
> There are times when you need to drink more on purpose.
> When it's hot. When you exercise hard. When you have a fever, vomiting or diarrhea.
> Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra water too.
> Older adults should drink regularly, because thirst may come late.
> But on the other hand, more is not always better.
> Remember the filter with a speed limit?
> If you drink too much, too fast, the extra water dilutes the sodium in your blood.
> This is called hyponatremia, and it can be dangerous.
> At the 2002 Boston Marathon, about thirteen percent of the runners who were tested had hyponatremia.
> The risk was highest in those who gained weight during the race.
> In other words, they drank more than they sweated out.
> And if you have heart or kidney disease, follow your doctor's advice on how much to drink.

## Cảnh 17 · Elimination
Hình: BẢNG: ba hàng A/B/C; gạch A, gạch C, khoanh B.
> Now let's rule out the options one by one.
> Option A: a fixed eight glasses. There's no solid basis for this number, and everyone's needs are different. Out.
> Option C: the more, the better. Drinking too much can lead to hyponatremia. Out.
> That leaves option B: listen to your thirst, drink more when you need to, and watch the color of your pee.

## Cảnh 18 · The answer
Hình: Chữ "B" lớn → van-phong.png: cậu bé (uong-nuoc) uống vài ngụm; rồi (vui) chai nước cạnh màn hình.
> The answer is B.
> Back to three in the afternoon. You take a few sips of water, then one more glass before your presentation.
> No need to force down a whole liter at once.
> From tomorrow, you keep the bottle where you can see it, and drink whenever you feel thirsty.
> A few hours later, your pee is back to a pale straw yellow.

## Cảnh 19 · The surprise
Hình: BẢNG: viên sỏi thận; hai nhóm (99 / 100), thanh "12%" và "27%"; "5 YEARS · ITALY · 1996"; cậu bé (ngac-nhien).
> And there's one more surprising benefit.
> In 1996, Italian scientists followed nearly two hundred people who had had kidney stones, for five years.
> One group was told to drink enough water to pee at least two liters a day.
> After five years, only about twelve percent of that group had stones come back.
> In the other group, the number was twenty-seven percent.
> The American Urological Association's 2014 guideline advises people who've had stones to drink enough.
> Enough to pee at least two and a half liters a day.
