## Cảnh 01 · Title
Thời lượng: 4
Hình: Thẻ giấy: "Why can't you focus on reading this?" — chữ hơi rung, đèn rọi quét qua.

## Cảnh 02 · Opening story
Hình: van-phong.jpg — cậu bé ngồi đọc; điện thoại rung; đồng hồ; các tab trình duyệt.
> Two in the afternoon. You have one article to read before a meeting.
> Just twelve pages. How hard can it be?
> You read the first paragraph. Then your phone buzzes.
> It's nothing. Just a sale on shoes you'll never buy.
> You go back to the article. Wait, where were you?
> You read the same paragraph again. And again.
> Your eyes reach the bottom of the page, but your mind is thinking about dinner.
> You open a new tab, just to check one thing.
> Forty minutes later, you're still on page two.
> You scroll back up and realize you can't remember a single thing from page one.
> Is something wrong with your brain?

## Cảnh 03 · The goldfish claim
Hình: Bể cá vàng; nhãn "HUMANS: 8 SECONDS?" vs "GOLDFISH: 9 SECONDS?"; dấu hỏi; sau đó tem "NO STUDY BEHIND IT".
> You may have heard a scary fact.
> Humans now have an attention span of eight seconds, shorter than a goldfish.
> It's been repeated in news stories, talks and ads all over the world.
> But when BBC reporters went looking for the study behind it, they couldn't find one.
> The report that spread the number didn't contain the research behind it.
> And nobody had measured a goldfish's attention span either.
> So if it's not a goldfish brain, what's going on?

## Cảnh 04 · Clue one: the wandering mind
Hình: BẢNG: điện thoại hỏi ngẫu nhiên "What are you thinking about?"; vòng tròn 47%; sau đó trang sách với mắt chạy dọc chữ nhưng bong bóng nghĩ về đồ ăn; nhãn "≈ 1 IN 5 CHECK-INS".
> Let's collect some clues.
> Clue one. In a famous study, an app pinged thousands of people at random moments and asked what they were thinking about.
> Nearly half the time, forty-seven percent, they were thinking about something other than what they were doing.
> Reading is no exception.
> When researchers interrupt people reading a story and ask, "Were you on task?", the answer is often no. In several studies, around one in five times.
> And here's the strange part. During these moments, the eyes keep moving across the words.
> Your eyes are reading. Your mind is not.
> Your eyes even stop slowing down for hard or unusual words, as if they were on autopilot.
> And readers who zone out more often do worse on questions about the text afterward.

## Cảnh 05 · Clue two: the buzz
Hình: BẢNG: người làm bài kiểm tra chú ý trên máy tính; điện thoại úp bàn rung; không chạm vào; số lỗi tăng; nhãn "JUST THE BUZZ".
> Clue two. In a 2015 experiment, people did a task that needed close attention.
> Some of them got a text message or a call on their phone during the task.
> They didn't answer it. They didn't even touch the phone.
> Yet they made noticeably more mistakes, about as many as people who actually used their phones.

## Cảnh 06 · Clue three: the switch
Hình: BẢNG: hai thẻ việc A/B; mũi tên qua lại; đồng hồ cát "SWITCH COST"; sau đó bóng mờ của việc cũ lơ lửng "ATTENTION RESIDUE".
> Clue three. Every time you switch from one task to another, your brain pays a small time cost to change its rules.
> The more complex the tasks, the bigger the cost.
> And when you switch away from something unfinished, part of your attention stays stuck on it.
> Researchers call this attention residue.

## Cảnh 07 · Clue four: paper or screen
Hình: BẢNG: sách giấy vs máy tính bảng; cột hiểu bài nhỉnh hơn ở giấy; nhãn "SMALL EDGE FOR PAPER" và "SCREEN READERS: MORE OVERCONFIDENT".
> Clue four. A large review compared reading on paper with reading on screens.
> On average, people understood informational texts a little better on paper.
> The gap was bigger when they were in a hurry.
> And people reading on screens were more likely to overestimate how much they'd understood.

## Cảnh 08 · The question
Hình: BẢNG: ba phương án A/B/C có biểu tượng.
> So why can't you focus on reading?
> A: your attention span has shrunk to less than a goldfish's.
> B: your phone has permanently damaged your brain.
> Or C: your attention is like a spotlight that keeps getting pulled away, and every trip back costs you a little.

## Cảnh 09 · Take a guess
Thời lượng: 10
Hình: Thẻ tóm tắt bốn manh mối + ba phương án + đếm ngược 3‑2‑1.
> Pause the video and take a guess.

## Cảnh 10 · The key
Hình: Cậu bé trong bong bóng suy nghĩ; bình "ATTENTION TANK" bị gạch, đổi thành đèn rọi.
> The key is this. Attention isn't a tank that slowly runs empty.
> It's more like a spotlight that you keep aiming, and that keeps getting pulled off target.
> To see why reading suffers so much, let's look at what reading asks of that spotlight.

## Cảnh 11 · Reading is a chain
Hình: BẢNG: các câu nối thành chuỗi; "IDEA 1 → IDEA 2 → IDEA 3"; khi đứt một mắt xích, quay lại đọc lại.
> Reading isn't just seeing words.
> To understand a paragraph, your brain holds on to the last few ideas and links each new sentence to them.
> It builds a little model of what the text is saying.
> That chain is fragile.
> If your spotlight drifts for even a few sentences, a link goes missing.
> Then the next sentence makes no sense, and you have to go back.
> That's why you read the same paragraph three times.

## Cảnh 12 · Pull number one: your own mind
Hình: BẢNG: não với vùng "DEFAULT MODE NETWORK" sáng lên khi trôi; văn khó → trôi nhiều hơn; nhãn "YOU OFTEN DON'T NOTICE".
> The first thing pulling your spotlight is your own mind.
> When your attention drifts inward, a set of brain areas called the default mode network becomes more active.
> It's busy with memories, plans and daydreams.
> Mind wandering isn't a defect. It helps you plan and imagine.
> But while reading, it quietly steals the thread.
> And often you don't notice until a page later.
> The harder or more boring the text, the more often the mind slips away.

## Cảnh 13 · Pull number two: cues
Hình: BẢNG: điện thoại phát sóng rung; mũi tên kéo đèn rọi; tuần bật thông báo vs tuần tắt; nhãn "MORE INATTENTION WITH ALERTS ON"; sau đó điện thoại nằm im với dấu "?" và tem "DIDN'T REPLICATE".
> The second pull comes from cues around you.
> A buzz or a ping is designed to grab attention, and it works.
> Even if you ignore it, your mind starts wondering who it was.
> In one experiment, students spent one week with phone alerts on and another with alerts off.
> With alerts on, they reported more inattention.
> What about a silent phone just sitting on your desk?
> A 2017 study claimed that even its mere presence drains your brainpower.
> But later, careful attempts to repeat that study did not find the effect.
> So it's the buzzing, more than the phone itself, that we have good evidence against.

## Cảnh 14 · Pull number three: switching
Hình: BẢNG: thanh tab trình duyệt; mỗi lần chuyển, đèn rọi phải "khởi động lại"; bóng mờ việc cũ; nhãn "EACH RETURN COSTS".
> The third pull is switching.
> Opening a new tab feels like it takes one second.
> But coming back means reloading the chain of ideas you were holding.
> Part of your mind is still on the email you didn't finish.
> Do that twenty times, and forty minutes disappear.

## Cảnh 15 · Pull number four: screens
Hình: BẢNG: máy tính bảng với ngón tay lướt nhanh; mắt nhảy cóc theo hình chữ F; nhãn "SKIM MODE?"; tem "STILL BEING TESTED".
> The fourth pull may be the screen itself.
> We do a lot of quick, shallow reading on screens: messages, feeds, headlines.
> One idea is that we carry that skimming habit over to everything we read on a screen.
> We read faster, and we check our own understanding less.
> That could explain why screen readers in the review felt more confident than they should have.
> But this is still an idea being tested, not a settled fact.

## Cảnh 16 · Long stretches
Hình: BẢNG: đường hiệu suất tụt dần theo thời gian (nhóm không nghỉ) vs đường phẳng (nhóm có nghỉ ngắn); nhãn "BRIEF BREAKS".
> There's one more thing.
> When people do a single focused task for a long time, their performance tends to slip.
> In a small 2011 study, people who took two very short breaks from a fifty-minute task didn't show that slip.
> So stepping away for a moment, on purpose, can help you come back sharper.

## Cảnh 17 · Is multitasking rewiring you?
Hình: BẢNG: người dùng nhiều màn hình cùng lúc; biểu đồ năm 2009 cột lớn, rồi các nghiên cứu sau cột nhỏ dần; nhãn "SMALL LINK · STILL UNCLEAR".
> What about people who juggle many screens all day?
> A well-known 2009 study found that heavy media multitaskers were worse at ignoring distractions.
> It made headlines.
> But when other scientists tried to repeat it, the results were mixed.
> Pooling many studies, the link turned out to be small, and it may be even smaller than it looks.
> So we can't say that multitasking has rewired anyone's brain.

## Cảnh 18 · Can you train it?
Hình: BẢNG: hai lớp học — "MINDFULNESS" vs "NUTRITION"; sau hai tuần, nhóm chánh niệm ít trôi hơn, điểm đọc hiểu cao hơn; nhãn "SMALL STUDY · 48 STUDENTS".
> Can you train your spotlight?
> In a 2013 study, forty-eight students took a two-week class.
> Half learned mindfulness: noticing when the mind drifts and gently bringing it back.
> The other half took a class on nutrition.
> Afterward, the mindfulness group's minds wandered less, and they scored higher on a reading comprehension test.
> It was a small study, but it suggests that noticing the drift is a skill you can practice.

## Cảnh 19 · The answer
Hình: Phương án C sáng lên, A và B bị gạch; nhãn "SPOTLIGHT".
> So, back to the question.
> The answer is C.
> The eight-second goldfish fact has no study behind it.
> And there's no solid evidence that your phone has permanently broken your brain.
> Your attention is a spotlight, and today's world keeps tugging it away.
> And it's not just you. Remember, people's minds drift nearly half the time, whatever they're doing.

## Cảnh 20 · How to hold the spotlight
Hình: BẢNG: năm mẹo dạng biểu tượng hiện lần lượt.
> The good news is that you can make the spotlight easier to hold.
> Turn off notifications while you read, and put your phone out of reach.
> Keep one tab open. One task at a time.
> For text that really matters, consider paper, and give yourself enough time.
> When you notice you've drifted, go back to the last sentence you remember, not the whole page.
> And take short, planned breaks before your focus runs down.

> Phone on silent, in the drawer. One tab.
