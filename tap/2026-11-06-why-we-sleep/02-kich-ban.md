## Cảnh 02 · Opening story
Hình: phong-ngu.jpg (đêm) — cậu bé bên bàn học, đồng hồ, cốc cà phê; sáng hôm sau lop-hoc-bang.jpg.
> Nine at night. Your big exam is tomorrow morning.
> There are still three chapters you haven't even opened.
> So you make a plan. No sleep tonight.
> You pour a big mug of coffee and open the first book.
> Midnight. Two in the morning. The words start to swim on the page.
> At six, the sky turns gray. You've read everything once.
> At nine, you sit down in the exam room and turn over the paper.
> And the answer you read at three in the morning is simply gone.
> You feel foggy, grumpy, and strangely slow.
> Three days later, you come down with a cold.
> Was that all-nighter really worth it?

## Cảnh 03 · The puzzle
Hình: Cậu bé suy nghĩ, chiếc bánh tròn chia 3 phần: 1/3 tô màu đêm "ASLEEP"; bên cạnh bong bóng "WASTED TIME?".
> Here's a strange fact. You spend about a third of your life asleep.
> Lying still, eyes closed, unable to react to danger.
> For an animal in the wild, that sounds like a terrible idea.
> And yet, scientists have never found an animal that clearly doesn't sleep.
> So sleep must be doing something important.
> But what, exactly?

## Cảnh 04 · Clue one: the brain at night
Hình: BẢNG: đường sóng điện não lúc thức (nhỏ, nhanh) → ngủ sâu (lớn, chậm) → REM (giống lúc thức); nhãn "AWAKE", "DEEP SLEEP", "REM".
> Let's collect some clues.
> Clue one. Put sensors on someone's head while they sleep, and you can watch their brain waves.
> In deep sleep, the waves become slow and huge, like a whole stadium chanting together.
> Later in the night comes REM sleep, short for rapid eye movement.
> That's when most vivid dreams happen.
> And during REM, the brain is about as active as when you're awake.
> So the brain doesn't simply switch off at night.

## Cảnh 05 · Clue two: memory
Hình: BẢNG: hai nhóm học cùng một bài; nhóm ngủ — sách cất vào tủ; nhóm thức trắng — ô nhớ nứt; nhãn "≈ 19% FEWER NEW MEMORIES" · Yoo et al., 2007.
> Clue two. Learn something, then sleep on it, and you usually remember it better than if you'd stayed awake for the same number of hours.
> Clue three. In one experiment, people stayed awake all night, then tried to learn a set of pictures the next day.
> Two days later, they remembered about nineteen percent less than people who had slept normally.

## Cảnh 06 · Clue four: colds
Hình: BẢNG: hai người, nhỏ giọt virus vào mũi; nhóm ngủ < 6 giờ hắt hơi; nhãn "≈ 4× MORE LIKELY TO CATCH A COLD" · Prather et al., 2015.
> Clue four. Researchers measured how long people slept for a week.
> Then they dripped a cold virus into their noses, on purpose, and kept them in quarantine.
> People who had slept less than six hours a night were about four times more likely to catch the cold than those who slept more than seven.

## Cảnh 07 · The question
Hình: BẢNG: ba phương án A/B/C có biểu tượng.
> So what is sleep mainly for?
> A: your body powers down to save energy, like a phone on low battery.
> B: your brain switches off to rest, like a car parked in the garage.
> Or C: your brain and body start a night shift, filing memories, doing maintenance, and building defenses.

## Cảnh 09 · The key
Hình: Cậu bé trong bong bóng suy nghĩ; công tắc "OFF" bị gạch, đổi thành biển "NIGHT SHIFT".
> The key is this. Sleep isn't an off switch. It's a different work mode.
> To see what that work is, let's start with how you fall asleep in the first place.

## Cảnh 10 · Sleep pressure
Hình: BẢNG: bình chứa "ADENOSINE" đầy dần theo giờ thức (7 AM → 11 PM); thụ thể trong não; phân tử cà phê chặn thụ thể.
> From the moment you wake up, a chemical called adenosine slowly builds up in your brain.
> The longer you stay awake, the more there is, and the sleepier you feel.
> Scientists call this sleep pressure.
> While you sleep, the adenosine is cleared away, and you wake up refreshed.
> Caffeine works by sitting in adenosine's parking spots in the brain.
> The adenosine is still there. You just can't feel it.
> And caffeine lingers. For many adults, half of it is still in the body four to six hours later.
> In one study, a strong dose of caffeine taken six hours before bed cut sleep by about an hour.

## Cảnh 11 · The body clock
Hình: BẢNG: đồng hồ 24 giờ, mặt trời/mặt trăng; vùng nhỏ trong não "MASTER CLOCK"; đường melatonin lên buổi tối; ánh sáng chặn.
> The second force is your body clock.
> Deep in your brain, a tiny master clock runs on a cycle of about twenty-four hours and eleven minutes.
> Every morning, daylight resets it to match the sun.
> In the evening, as it gets dark, your body releases melatonin, a hormone that signals that night has come.
> When high sleep pressure meets the clock's night signal, you fall asleep easily.
> Bright screens late at night can blur that signal.

## Cảnh 12 · A night in cycles
Hình: BẢNG: biểu đồ giấc ngủ (hypnogram) 8 giờ, 5 chu kỳ; ngủ sâu đầu đêm, REM cuối đêm; vùng cuối bị cắt khi dậy sớm 2 giờ.
> Once you're asleep, your brain doesn't stay in one state.
> It moves through cycles of roughly ninety minutes, four to six times a night.
> Early in the night, the cycles are full of deep sleep.
> Toward morning, REM sleep takes over and gets longer.
> So if you cut your night short by two hours, you don't lose a little of everything.
> You lose a big chunk of your REM sleep.

## Cảnh 13 · Job one: memory
Hình: BẢNG: hồ thủy sinh "HIPPOCAMPUS" (hộp thư tạm) → tủ hồ sơ "CORTEX"; trong giấc ngủ sâu thẻ trí nhớ bay sang tủ; hộp thư được dọn trống.
> Now, the first job of the night shift: memory.
> During the day, new experiences land in a small brain area called the hippocampus, like an inbox.
> During deep sleep, the brain replays the day's memories and moves the important ones into long-term storage.
> That's why sleeping after learning helps you remember.
> Many researchers think it also clears room in the inbox, ready for tomorrow.
> Skip the night, and the inbox stays full.
> That's what happened in clue three. New memories had nowhere to go.
> REM sleep seems to help too, linking new memories with old ones and smoothing out emotional ones.

## Cảnh 14 · Job two: cleaning
Hình: BẢNG: lát cắt não chuột, khe giữa tế bào nở rộng ~60% khi ngủ, dòng dịch cuốn rác (beta-amyloid); sau đó não người, sóng chậm và dòng dịch não tủy; nhãn "STILL DEBATED".
> The second job may be cleaning.
> Your brain cells produce waste as they work, including a protein called beta-amyloid.
> In a 2013 study in mice, the spaces between brain cells grew by about sixty percent during sleep.
> Fluid flowed through more easily and washed out more of that waste.
> In 2019, brain scans of sleeping people showed waves of fluid pulsing through the brain in step with deep sleep's slow waves.
> But a 2024 mouse study found the opposite result, so scientists are still arguing about how this cleaning works.

## Cảnh 15 · Job three: defense
Hình: BẢNG: lá chắn miễn dịch; ống tiêm vắc-xin cúm; hai cột kháng thể: ngủ đủ vs 4 giờ/đêm (một nửa).
> The third job is defense.
> Remember the cold virus experiment? Short sleepers caught the cold far more often.
> Sleep also affects vaccines.
> In one small study, young men slept only four hours a night for several nights around the time of a flu shot.
> Ten days later, they had about half as many antibodies as men who had slept normally.

## Cảnh 16 · The hidden cost
Hình: BẢNG: biểu đồ 14 ngày, đường lỗi chú ý của nhóm 6 giờ tăng dần; đường "cảm thấy buồn ngủ" gần phẳng; sau đó xe hơi + "4–5 HOURS → ≈ 4× CRASH RISK".
> What if you sleep just a bit less, every night?
> In one experiment, people spent just six hours in bed each night for two weeks.
> Their attention slips kept climbing, day after day.
> By the end, their performance was close to that of people kept awake for a whole night.
> But they only felt a little sleepy. They didn't realize how impaired they were.
> On the road, drivers who'd slept only four to five hours in the past day had about four times the crash risk of those who'd slept seven or more.

## Cảnh 17 · Night owls and teens
Hình: BẢNG: ba đồng hồ — chim sớm (6 giờ), cú đêm (muộn), tuổi teen (kim dịch muộn ~2 giờ); nhãn "HARD TO SLEEP BEFORE ≈ 11 PM"; trường học "START ≥ 8:30 AM" · AAP 2014.
> Your body clock isn't set the same as everyone else's.
> Some people are natural early birds. Others are night owls.
> And during puberty, the body clock shifts later, by up to about two hours.
> Many teenagers find it hard to fall asleep before eleven at night.
> That's one reason the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that middle and high schools start no earlier than eight thirty in the morning.
> So a teenager who can't wake up at seven isn't always lazy. Their body clock is still set to night.

## Cảnh 18 · Can you catch up?
Hình: BẢNG: lịch tuần — 5 đêm 5 giờ (đỏ), cuối tuần ngủ thoải mái (xanh), rồi lại đỏ; nhãn "+ ≈ 1 H CAUGHT UP"; đường đường huyết xấu đi khi quay lại ngủ ít · Depner et al., 2019.
> What about sleeping in on the weekend to pay back the debt?
> In one 2019 study, people got about five hours of sleep a night during the week.
> On the weekend, they could sleep as much as they wanted.
> But they only caught up about an hour in total.
> And when the short nights started again, their bodies handled sugar even worse.
> Sleep works best on a steady schedule, not as a loan you pay back on Saturday.

## Cảnh 19 · The answer
Hình: Phương án C sáng lên, A và B bị gạch; nhãn "NIGHT SHIFT".
> So, back to the question.
> The answer is C.
> Sleep does save a bit of energy, but that can't explain the memory and immune effects.
> And the brain clearly doesn't switch off.
> Sleep is an active night shift for your brain and body.

## Cảnh 20 · How much do you need?
Hình: BẢNG: thanh 7+ giờ cho người lớn; 1/3 người lớn Mỹ ngủ ít hơn; năm mẹo dạng biểu tượng.
> So how much do you need?
> Sleep experts recommend at least seven hours a night for adults.
> Yet in the United States, about one in three adults regularly gets less.
> A few habits help.
> Wake up at about the same time every day, even on weekends.
> Get daylight in the morning, and dim the lights at night.
> Keep caffeine to the morning and early afternoon.
> Keep your bedroom dark and cool.
> And don't use alcohol as a sleep aid. It may knock you out, but it breaks up your sleep later in the night.

> Study, then go to bed.
> Let the night shift file what you learned.
> The next morning, the answers are where you left them.
